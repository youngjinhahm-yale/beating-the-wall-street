Beating the Wall Street

Youngjin Hahm · MGT 409 AI Foundations for Managers · Final Project · October 2026

Repository: github.com/youngjinhahm-yale/beating-the-wall-street (runs locally with python run.py; see the README)

One sentence: a daily news-prediction game in which AI agents turn yesterday's market news into Up / Flat / Down bets, so retail investors practice calling the market against a crowd and turn every result into a written, scored rule.

1. Motivation

Retail investors do not lose to institutions because they lack news. They lose because they never build a learning curve.

Who has the problem. Self-directed investors who trade on phone apps (Robinhood, Toss, Webull) and follow market news every day. I am one of them. Like most of my friends who invest, I read headlines, act on a feeling, and move on. When a trade works I do not know whether I was right or lucky; when it fails I blame the market. There is no record of why I acted, so the same mistake comes back.

How institutions differ. A fund desk writes its reasoning before the trade, has risk rules (position limits, loss cuts), and reviews outcomes against those rules afterwards. Each trade becomes feedback and the rules improve. Retail investors have none of that structure, and studying alone rarely fixes it: reading about markets is passive, so most people quit within weeks.

What it costs today.

  • Money: impulsive, headline-driven trades that no written rule would have allowed.
  • Time: 30–60 minutes a day of scattered news reading that never becomes a reusable lesson (my own habit; to be confirmed in user interviews).
  • Frustration: no way to separate skill from luck, which pushes people to quit or to over-trade.

Why it matters. If practice is fun enough to repeat daily and gives honest feedback before any money is at risk, retail investors can build the one habit that separates professionals from gamblers: rules that are written down, tested and scored.

2. What is new here

Nothing on the market combines a daily commit-before-the-outcome bet on real news, crowd odds, AI explanations of what really moved the market, and a personal rulebook with scored win rates. Each existing product covers one piece:

ProductWhat it doesWhat it lacks for building a learning curve
Investopedia Stock SimulatorPaper trading with virtual cashNo daily news focus; no feedback on why a trade worked
Wall Street SurvivorPaper trading plus finance coursesCourses are separate from the live market; no daily loop
Kalshi / PolymarketPrediction markets, including index rangesReal money and regulation; built for profit, not practice; no explanation of outcomes

Morning Brew / Bloomberg newslettersDaily market summariesPassive reading: no prediction, no feedback
ChatGPTExplains any headline on requestExplains after the fact; no commitment, no score, no memory of your mistakes

What this project does differently:

  1. Commit, then see. Bets lock at 9:25 AM ET, before the market opens, and settle on the official open-to-close move. The outcome is real feedback, not hindsight.
  2. Crowd odds teach "priced in". Payouts are parimutuel (like horse racing): a correct call against the crowd pays more, so players learn that the consensus is already in the price.
  3. Skill by type of news. League-of-Legends-style tiers are kept separately for Macro, Company, Geopolitics and Sector news, so players see where their edge is.
  4. Rules, scored. After each close the Coach agent writes one testable rule per card; players save rules and the app tracks each rule's win rate.
  5. Big-move replays. Agents detect sharp intraday turns in 1-minute index data and attach the headline that caused them, giving unlimited practice on real moments.

3. The agents

Six PydanticAI agents do only language work: reading, writing and judging text. Deterministic Python code runs the schedule, the money (points) and all arithmetic, so the system is predictable and cheap.

Organization. A pipeline, not a free-form team: each agent hands a typed Pydantic object to the next step, and code decides what runs when. The only loop is a single Editor retry for cards the Fact-checker failed. Agents that read untrusted text (Editor, Fact-checker, Moderator, personal Coach) get no tools, so an injected instruction has nothing to act with. The tools that do exist live on one read-only FastMCP server.

Model choice. Every agent uses gpt-6-luna through Portkey, the only model allowed on the course key. The tasks are short, structured and well-specified, which suits a small, cheap model. Static prompts sit first and carry a prompt-cache breakpoint, so repeated calls read them at the cached price.

Even a 10,000-member brokerage education program would cost about $61 a month in model fees. At that scale the binding costs are hosting and moderation staff, not tokens.

What I did to keep cost down

  1. Deterministic orchestration. Python, not an LLM "boss", sequences agents: no tokens spent on routing.
  2. Code pre-filter. Keyword scoring and de-duplication cut ~165 headlines to ~37 before the Scout sees them.
  3. Prompt caching. Static instructions first, with a cache breakpoint: 111,387 input tokens were read from cache, saving $0.0100 (9% of total spend so far).
  4. Shared explanations. One post-mortem per card serves every player; personal coaching runs only when a player taps "Ask Coach".
  5. Zero-token features. Settlement, payouts, League Points, tiers, the inflection scan, missions and the live tracker are plain code.
  6. Small model, short outputs. gpt-6-luna with typed outputs and length limits (titles ≤ 60 chars, one-sentence summaries).

5. Safety

The risks that matter here: poisoned text (news or posts) steering an agent, invented facts or answer leaks on a card, runaway agents, overspending, and cheating on bets.

RiskDefense built
Prompt injection in newsHeadlines that look like instructions are quarantined in code before any model sees them; all untrusted text is fenced as data; agents reading it have no tools; the model provider's own content filter is a further layer.
Prompt injection / spam in postsCode rule filter (injection, links, all-caps), then the Moderator agent; posts fail closed (held) if moderation is unavailable.
Invented factsFact-checker verifies every claim against the cited headlines; failed cards get one rewrite, then are dropped.
Answer leakageDirection-word check in code plus the Fact-checker. Across all runs it failed 29 of 85 draft cards (34%), e.g. "Meta shares fell 7%".
History leakArchive headlines often carry only a date. History runs cut the news window at midnight ET and drop headlines that name the trade date — added after I caught a real leak in testing.
Runaway agentsMax 8 model calls and 12 tool calls per agent run.
OverspendingDaily token caps (600k for all agents, 40k per player); manual pipeline runs rate-limited to one per 30 minutes; when a cap trips, AI features pause and the game keeps working.
Cheating / bad settlementServer-side 09:25 ET lock; settlement in code from official prices; a missing price voids the card and refunds stakes.
Financial-advice framingPoints only, no cash value; agents explain the past and never recommend trades; "education only" notice on every page.

6. Impact

The app has run on real data for four weeks: 18 rounds from 2026-09-28 to 2026-10-23, 48 settled daily cards and 6,860 stored headlines, at a total AI cost of $0.11.

What the data already teaches

  • News rarely explains the day by itself. The Coach judged that the card's own news drove the move in only 7 of 48 settled cards; "partly" in 15; and something else (usually a market-wide move) in 26. This is exactly the lesson the project is about: a headline-driven bet is often a bet on the market, not on the news.
  • The odds are honest. Outcomes were Up 23, Flat 6, Down 19, against 18 / 10 / 20 expected from the historical base rates the app uses to seed its pools.
  • Attribution stays honest. Of 22 sharp intraday moves, the Analyst gave a medium-confidence cause for 7 and answered "no clear cause" for 12 rather than inventing one.

7. Limits and next steps

What does not work yet

  • It needs to be running. Rounds are built and settled only while the app runs; there is no hosted version yet, so players must use the computer it runs on.
  • Thin crowd. With few players the odds come mostly from the house seed (historical base rates), so the "beat the crowd" mechanic is weak until more people play.
  • Historical attribution is coarse. Free news archives often have date-only timestamps, so past big moves rarely get a confident cause. Live RSS polling fixes this going forward.
  • Daily moves are noisy. One day's open-to-close move is mostly market-wide; a single card is weak evidence of skill. Tiers need many calls to mean something.
  • Data dependency. Prices come from Yahoo Finance's public chart endpoint, which is unofficial and could change.

With another month

  1. Host it (Render or Railway) with reminders at 6:00 AM ("round open") and 4:15 PM ("results are in").
  2. Private leagues with invite codes, for clubs and friend groups.
  3. "Your pattern" insights from bet history (e.g. "you pick Up 78% of the time").
  4. Swipe-to-bet on mobile and a weekly season with leaderboard resets.
  5. Score rules across all players, so the best community rules surface automatically.
